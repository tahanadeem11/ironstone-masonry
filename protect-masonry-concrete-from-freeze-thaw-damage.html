How to Protect Masonry, Walkways and Concrete from Freeze-Thaw Damage in CT & NY

  1. Home
  2. Blog
  3. Protect Masonry from Freeze-Thaw Damage
By Ironstone Masonry November 20, 2026 6 min read Maintenance

Key Takeaways

  • Freeze-thaw damage happens when water enters masonry, freezes, expands about 9%, and cracks the material from inside.
  • Watch for flaking surfaces (spalling), crumbling or recessed mortar joints, hairline cracks and heaving pavers or slabs.
  • The best protection is keeping water away: clean gutters, good grading, sealed surfaces and repaired joints before the first hard freeze.
  • Avoid rock salt on concrete and stone; use sand or gentler de-icers, and schedule repairs when temperatures allow materials to cure.

If you own a home in Connecticut or New York, you already know winter is hard on a property. Brick, stone, concrete and pavers are tough materials, but they all share one weakness: water. Understanding how winter moisture damages masonry is the first step to protecting your investment.

How freeze-thaw damage happens

Masonry materials are porous. Rain and melting snow soak into tiny pores and gaps. When temperatures drop below freezing, that water turns to ice and expands by roughly nine percent. The expansion pushes outward from the inside. When the ice thaws, the water drains and the material is left a little weaker. Our region can swing above and below freezing many times in a single season, so the damage compounds year after year.

What the damage looks like

  • Spalling: the face of the brick, stone or concrete flakes or peels away.
  • Crumbling or recessed mortar joints: mortar that is sandy, cracked or sunken lets water in.
  • Hairline and widening cracks in slabs, steps and walls.
  • Heaving or settling: pavers or slabs that sit uneven after winter.
  • Efflorescence: a white, chalky deposit that shows moisture is moving through the masonry.

Prevention before winter

  1. Move water away from masonry. Clean gutters and extend downspouts so roof runoff does not pour onto walls, steps or walkways.
  2. Check grading. Soil and hardscape should slope away from the house and foundation so water does not pool.
  3. Repair joints and small cracks now. Repointing worn mortar and filling hairline cracks is far cheaper than repairing the damage they cause after a winter of freezing.
  4. Consider a breathable sealer. A penetrating sealer helps repel water while still letting trapped moisture escape. Avoid film-forming coatings that can trap moisture inside.
  5. Re-sand paver joints. Fresh joint sand keeps pavers locked together and slows water entry.
  6. Trim vegetation and fix irrigation leaks that keep masonry constantly damp.

De-icing the right way

Rock salt (sodium chloride) is the most common ice melter, and it is hard on concrete, mortar and some natural stone because it increases the number of freeze-thaw cycles at the surface and can speed up scaling. Safer habits include:

  • Shovel promptly so less ice forms in the first place.
  • Use sand or grit for traction instead of salt where possible.
  • If you need a de-icer, look for calcium magnesium acetate or products labeled safe for concrete and pavers.
  • Keep salt off newly poured concrete for its first winter.
  • Sweep up residue in spring so salts do not stay in the surface.

Repairs and the best time to do them

Mortar and concrete need to cure properly, which is why most masonry repairs are best done from spring through early fall when temperatures stay above roughly 40°F. Late fall is the time to assess and plan so repairs can begin as soon as conditions allow. Typical fixes include:

  • Repointing (tuckpointing): grinding out deteriorated mortar and replacing it with fresh, compatible mortar.
  • Replacing spalled brick or stone that has lost its face.
  • Resetting pavers and walkways that have heaved or sunk, and rebuilding the base beneath them.
  • Concrete crack repair and resurfacing where the slab is still structurally sound.

Frequently asked questions

What causes brick to flake or peel in winter?

Water absorbed by the brick freezes and expands, pushing the surface layer off. This is called spalling, and it gets worse each winter if moisture is not controlled.

When should mortar joints be repaired?

When mortar is cracked, crumbling or noticeably recessed, it should be repointed. Doing it before winter keeps water out of the wall during the next freeze.

Is it safe to use rock salt on a concrete walkway?

It is best avoided. Rock salt can cause surface scaling and wear on concrete and pavers. Sand or concrete-safe de-icers are gentler choices.

Can masonry repairs be done in winter?

Emergency safety fixes can be handled, but most repairs are best done when temperatures stay above about 40°F so mortar and concrete can cure correctly.
